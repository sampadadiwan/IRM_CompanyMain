Data strategy

Your data lake is waiting on your operations.

Large funds buy a data lake to finally see all their data in one place. But fund data is small, it has to be loaded somewhere anyway, and a lake can't make the numbers right. Why the system of record comes first, and how it feeds the lake you may already have.

Nov 5, 2026 · 9 min read · Data strategy

Why large funds want a lake

The pitch is familiar. Load the administrator files, the general ledger, the bank statements, the CRM and the portfolio companies' KPI packs into Snowflake or Databricks, put Power BI on top, and you will finally have one source of truth.

The reasons behind it are real, and it is worth being fair about them:

  • Fragmentation. One or more administrators, a GL, a CRM, a portfolio monitoring tool and a great many spreadsheets. Each holds part of the picture, and none of them talk to each other. The lake is the first place they all meet.
  • Ownership. "We own our data, not the vendor." Nobody wants their fund history locked inside someone else's product.
  • A mandate. The board, or a consultant, asks for a "data strategy" or for the firm to be "AI-ready". A lake is a visible thing to buy.
  • Tools. Analysts want SQL, notebooks and their own dashboards, not a vendor's report screen.

Notice what is not on the list: analytics the firm cannot do today because the data is too big, or the queries too heavy. The lake is bought as an analytics project, but it is really an integration project. And integration is the part a lake does not do for you.

It isn't big data

Warehouses like Snowflake and Databricks are built for billions of rows: website clickstreams, device telemetry, market tick data. A large private-markets manager has a few thousand LPs, a few hundred portfolio companies, some dozens of vehicles and ten or fifteen years of quarters. Even the capital account ledger, the largest table any fund keeps, runs to millions of lines, not billions.

CapHive can hand an organisation its entire history as a single SQLite file. That is how big the problem is.

An ordinary relational database runs queries over data of this size comfortably. Scale is not why funds need a lake, and it is not what goes wrong when they build one.

The bill that never stops

What a fund does end up paying for is big-data infrastructure and the people to run it. The warehouse subscription is the line everyone sees, but it is rarely the largest:

  • The platform. Snowflake or Databricks compute and storage, billed on usage. Every dashboard refresh, every pipeline run and every analyst's query keeps the meter running.
  • The pipes. Ingestion tools to pull from each source, a transformation layer to model the data, an orchestrator to schedule it, monitoring to tell you when it breaks, and BI licences for everyone who reads the result.
  • The team. Data engineers to build and maintain the pipelines, analytics engineers to model the data, and someone who understands fund accounting well enough to tell them whether the numbers are right. Even a small team of two or three costs far more each year than the platform does.

The team is also permanent. The work is never finished, because the inputs keep changing. The administrator changes a file layout. A new fund launches with a different fee basis. A side letter adds an exception. A new vehicle sits above two others. Each change breaks a pipeline or a model, and the team re-implements in SQL logic that the fund already knows. When the one engineer who understood the carry model leaves, that knowledge leaves with them.

A permanent data team and a big-data platform, to manage a few gigabytes of numbers that still have to be reconciled by hand.

A system of record spends that money once, on getting the data right where it is created. The fund's rules live in one place, are maintained as configuration rather than code, and stay with the fund when people move on.

You have to load the data somewhere

Whichever way you go, someone has to collect the administrator's files, the GL extract, the bank statements, the subscription documents and the KPI packs, and load them. That work is the same. What differs is where the data lands, and what happens to it there.

In a lake, it lands as rows. What those rows mean is decided later, each time someone reads them. Every dashboard and notebook decides again what "called", "unfunded" or "NAV" means. Fee, carry and IRR logic is rewritten in SQL for each report, and no number points back to the rule that produced it.

A typical quarter-end. Two dashboards show different unfunded commitments for Fund II. One subtracts every call issued. The other subtracts only the calls that have been paid. Both queries are correct, they just answer different questions, and the data team spends a day finding out which one went to the LP advisory committee.

In a system of record, it lands in records that already know what they are. CapHive stores commitments, calls, remittances, distributions, units, cost lots and KPIs in tables built for them, plus whatever custom fields your funds need. Data is validated as it is imported: required fields, formats, ranges, duplicates. Fees, allocations and ratios are computed by one engine from formulas you can read, and every generated ledger line stores the formula and the input values that produced it. Access is set per fund, per investor and per document, and every change is logged with who made it and when.

The loading effort is the same. Only one of the two destinations makes the data trustworthy.

The lake wish-list, done in the system of record

The honest test is to take everything a fund hopes to get from a lake and ask whether it needs the lake. For a private-markets manager, almost none of it does.

Lakes are good at analytics, and that has not changed. The point is that this analytics does not need a second copy of the data that nobody checks. Done in the system of record, it runs on numbers that have already been validated, computed and approved.

What a correction costs

The difference shows most clearly when something is wrong. A side letter cut one LP's management fee from 2.0% to 1.75% from the first quarter, and nobody applied it. Two quarters later, someone notices.

Lake first

  1. Find every notebook, view and dashboard that computes fees.
  2. Patch the logic in each one, or in a shared transform, and backfill.
  3. Re-derive the LP's balances, ratios and statements further down the pipeline.
  4. Work out what the LP was already sent. The old values are gone unless someone kept snapshots.

System of record first

  1. Set the 1.75% rate on the LP's commitment.
  2. Unlock and re-run the two quarters. The fee entries are recomputed, and each one records the new rate in its working.
  3. Ratios, statements and the next capital call pick up the corrected ledger.
  4. The audit trail records who changed the rate and when, and the run history shows each re-run. Locked periods mean nothing else moves without someone deciding it should.

A lake keeps the latest answer. A system of record keeps the answer, the rule behind it, and what you told your investors along the way.

There are three ways out of CapHive, and a lake can use any of them:

  • Scheduled database dumps. A SQLite copy of your organisation's data, produced at the frequency you set (or on demand), stored as a document, and kept for a retention period you choose.
  • The REST API. The same endpoints the application uses, under the same permissions, so an integration sees exactly what that user could see on screen.
  • Report Builder exports. The reports your team already uses, exported for the warehouse to pick up.

Because every exported row carries the id of its source record, the warehouse can always trace a number back to where it came from. The data team stops rebuilding fee and IRR logic in SQL, and the numbers in the warehouse match the numbers your LPs received.

When you genuinely need a lake

Some firms do. A multi-strategy manager with public-markets trading data has a real big-data problem: tick data and order books do run to billions of rows. A group that mandates one lake across all its businesses, of which funds are only one, will have a lake whatever the fund team thinks. In both cases the argument still holds. Fund operations data should reach that lake from a system of record, not be rebuilt inside it.

Before you buy a lake

Six questions worth answering first:

  • Do your LP statements, notices and capital accounts come from one place today, or from spreadsheets around several systems?
  • If a fee rate turned out to be wrong, how many places would you have to fix?
  • Can you say, for any row, which investors are allowed to see it?
  • Is your data actually too big for an ordinary database, or just spread across too many systems?
  • Who will maintain the pipelines in year three, and what will that team cost every year after?
  • Is the data you want to analyse already checked anywhere, or will the lake be the first place it is checked?

If the answers point to operations rather than analytics, the lake can wait. Fix the system of record first. The lake, if you still want one, gets smaller, cheaper and more accurate as a result.
