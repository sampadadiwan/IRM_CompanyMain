Fund administrators, AI and the underlying infrastructure.

AI may be changing the expectation from weeks to minutes. But getting there isn't primarily an AI problem. It's an architecture problem.

Oct 1, 2026 · 3 min read · AI

We're not quite sure if AI will really be a risk to humanity. But it is certainly putting expectations of timelines for quarterly and monthly reporting at risk.

Fund managers are increasingly going to ask a very simple question:

“If AI can help send rockets to space, why does my investor reporting still take three weeks?”

And, increasingly, that expectation is not unreasonable. AI can dramatically compress the time required to draft commentary, analyse portfolio data, reconcile information, generate investor communications and assemble reporting packs.

But there is an important catch

Producing a report quickly is relatively easy. Producing it consistently, deterministically and with a complete audit trail is much harder. Ensuring every single allocation is done correctly, incorporating dynamic rules, excused investors and at times multi-vehicle and multi-currency computations, is harder still.

And every number ultimately needs to be traceable back to the underlying transaction, methodology and source data.

Why it's hard

Alternative funds are not standardised.

A single manager may operate across all of this at once.

01 · Structure

Funds, feeders and SPVs

Multiple vehicles, each with its own investors and rules.

02 · Currency

Currencies and accounting

Different currencies and accounting structures across vehicles.

03 · Allocation

Bespoke methodologies

Highly bespoke allocation methods, with excused investors and dynamic rules.

04 · Carry

Multi-stage waterfalls

Two- or three-stage carry waterfalls.

05 · LPs

LP-specific reporting

Different document and communication requirements across vehicles.

06 · Sources

Many sources of data

Portfolio information arriving from multiple sources and administrators.

The real opportunity

Not an LLM on top of spreadsheets. An operating architecture.

To deliver at enterprise grade, AI needs a ground-up data structure, with a context layer and definitions, so it can work with consistency and, more importantly, auditability.

  • Structured fund and investor data
  • Deterministic computation engines
  • Configurable workflows
  • Vehicle-level permissions and security
  • Complete audit trails
  • A data model that understands relationships across funds, investors, investments and transactions

Once that foundation exists

AI becomes extraordinarily powerful.

A quarterly reporting process can move from this…

collect→ reconcile→ calculate→ review→ draft→ format→ distribute

…towards something much closer to this:

Continuously structured data

Records stay current, so there is no collection step.

Deterministic computation

The same inputs give the same numbers, every time.

AI-assisted analysis

Commentary and analysis drafted on top of computed figures.

Review

People check the work rather than rebuild it.

Publish

Out the door, with the audit trail already written.

The role of the administrator

More important, not less.

The administrators that combine deep fund expertise with the right technology architecture will deliver something increasingly difficult to achieve manually: institutional-grade accuracy with near real-time responsiveness, without sacrificing customisation, control or auditability.

AI may be changing the expectation from weeks to minutes. But getting there isn't primarily an AI problem. It's an architecture problem.
